# Argument Review

Reviewed: `drafts/v1.md` against `thesis.md`. Reasoning only; technical accuracy is left to the Technical Critic.

**Verdict on the thesis: it does not survive as written, and this routes back to Gate 2, not to a prose edit.** The thesis bundles three claims. The taxonomy (emit / write / operate) survives in weakened form. "Design effort belongs at the boundary" survives, but it is the principle of least privilege and the audience already holds it. The load-bearing causal clause, "that boundary, not the model, is where reliability, accessibility and brand safety are settled," is not argued anywhere in the draft, and the draft's own findings contradict it. Because `thesis_approved` is true, the draft cannot quietly narrow the claim. The change needs re-approval (AGENTS.md).

## Strongest part of the argument

1. **"All four boundaries limit form or action. None of them judges content."** (end of Trade-offs, plus the last column of the boundary map.) This is the most specific, falsifiable and non-obvious statement in the piece. An experienced reader could disagree with it, and the table supports it for the Operate row, where the docs state the gap themselves. The draft buries it as the last line of a trade-offs section. It should be the spine of the piece. See "How the thesis could be stronger."
2. **The "Sensitive values never reach the model" paragraph** (Where the claims outrun the documents). It sets one announcement claim against specific documented mechanisms (`sendDataModel`, button actions that return resolved values) and then declines to overclaim ("The pages don't say whether..."). It is the only place where a vendor claim about a boundary is tested against that vendor's documentation.
3. **The honesty devices.** "Nothing was run first-hand," "my reading, not statements from the documents," and "I found no independent evaluation." They are good practice and fit this reader. They also expose the problem described under Weakest part.

## Weakest part

**"The boundary, not the model, decides reliability" is asserted, not argued. The draft's own evidence cuts against it.**

- The claim is stated in "The claim" and restated in the intro. No section compares the contribution of the boundary with the contribution of the model. No evidence about model behaviour is offered, and none could be, since nothing was run.
- The Trade-offs section concedes the opposite for the dominant failure mode: "An agent that chooses a registered component and fills it with wrong values has stayed inside every boundary described above." If the commonest failure (wrong content in a permitted component) sits inside the boundary, then reliability in that sense is not "settled" at the boundary. Whatever settles it is the model, a content check, or an evaluation, and the draft names none of them.
- The thesis lists three outcomes: reliability, accessibility and brand safety. The draft connects only brand safety (restyle the catalog) to the boundary. Accessibility appears in the Write section, where it comes from the component library's behaviors (what the agent builds *from*), and in the Trade-offs section, where the sandbox "says nothing about whether the interface it renders is accurate or accessible." Two of the three outcomes in the thesis are therefore shown to be outside the boundary, or not shown at all.
- **Reliability and safety are conflated.** A boundary caps the worst case (blast radius). The model sets the typical case (quality of component choice and values). The thesis uses "reliability" for both, and that equivocation lets the claim sound stronger than the evidence.

## Unsupported inference

1. **"Operate" and "Write" each rest on one project.** Evidence for Operate is UI SDK's `createAgent` plus WebMCP. Evidence for Write is UI SDK's skill. "The permission layer is the boundary" (Operate section) is generalized from one library's documentation. The three-job map therefore has five-plus projects behind one cell and one project behind two. The draft never says so. The heading "Boundary map" presents it as a field survey. Say plainly that two of the three jobs are evidenced by a single vendor's feature set, or find a second example.
2. **"Operate" is presented as a generative-UI job without defence.** Agents reading an accessibility or component snapshot and acting on a page are an established area (browser and computer-use agents). The draft includes it because UI SDK ships it, then says "Nothing here generates a new interface for an end user." If nothing is generated, why is it a job *called generative UI*? The draft's own sentence undercuts the premise. Either defend the inclusion or call it a neighbouring job.
3. **"Write" is a relabelling of build-time versus run-time.** The original Gate 2 thesis was "emit at runtime vs write at build time." The revision keeps that split and adds Operate. Then Trade-offs says writing is "the familiar kind of risk, and it comes with the familiar controls." If the risk and controls are familiar (code review, tests), the job has no distinctive boundary. The table's "Write" row ("Tested behaviors, tests, code review") describes ordinary engineering, not a boundary the developer draws around an agent. The three-boundaries claim in the thesis is really two plus ordinary review.
4. **The table column header "What the docs say it does not cover" is not what the cells contain.** Only the Operate cell is a documented statement. The Emit-unconstrained and Write cells are the author's reading (the draft admits this in a caveat under the table), and the Emit-constrained cell is an absence ("the A2UI pages describe no semantic check"). Absence in the pages read is treated as a capability gap in the system. Rename the column ("Gaps: documented / my reading / not found") so the evidence class is visible per cell. As labelled, the table lends documentary authority to the writer's conclusions.
5. **"Operating a live page is cheap to add and easy to over-trust."** (Trade-offs.) Neither half is supported by anything in the draft. No source says it is cheap, and no evidence shows people over-trust it. This reads as plausible-sounding intuition presented as a finding.
6. **Trade-offs follow from definitions, not evidence.** "Constrained emission buys predictability and costs expressiveness" is true by construction. "A sandbox limits what the code can reach" is the same. A senior reader gets nothing from the section except the final paragraph. It also omits costs this audience weighs: catalog maintenance and versioning (the draft's own A2UI 0.9-to-1.0 incompatibility evidence belongs here but sits in another section), agent/renderer drift, and who staffs the review for Write.

## Missing counterargument

1. **The catalog is partly a model-steering device, so "boundary versus model" is a false dichotomy.** The draft's own json-render code block gives each component a `description` ("A card container", "Display a metric value") and each action a description. Those strings are there for the model to read. The catalog is both a validation boundary and a prompt. Likewise the AI SDK variant lets "the model pick a tool." In constrained emission, boundary quality and model quality interact: a catalog with poor descriptions fails with a good model, and a good catalog is wasted on a weak one. Steelman: *the boundary is the model's interface, so you cannot design one without the other.* The draft never considers this, though it is visible in its own listing.
2. **No threat model, so "boundary" is not tied to what it defends against.** The three boundaries defend against different things: model error, an adversarial tool author (MCP Apps), and adversarial content steering the agent (Operate). Page content that manipulates an agent is the central risk for an agent that "can press buttons on your page," yet the draft does not name it. The `allow` callback and `data-agent-confirm` defend against that only if the developer writes the policy well, and the draft quotes the frank warning ("answers yes for the person") without drawing the conclusion. The boundary's strength then depends on the developer's policy and on user attention, which is not a property of a boundary. The strongest skeptic's question for the Operate section is "what attack or error does this stop?", and the draft does not answer it.
3. **Ownership is inconsistent with the thesis.** The thesis says "the boundary the developer owns." For MCP Apps, the draft's table says the boundary "Depends on the host enforcing the specification," and the objection response says that if MCP Apps wins "the boundary that matters will be the sandbox and the host's enforcement of it." An app author does not own that. The response therefore concedes the point rather than answering it: the more the field converges on the sandboxed-iframe model, the less the developer-owned-catalog framing applies. The draft treats this as a footnote to its objection section. It is a direct challenge to the "developer owns it" half of the thesis.
4. **The strongest objection is that the thesis is least privilege restated.** "Constrain an untrusted component at the trust boundary and test the boundary" is standard security engineering. The new content would be the mapping of jobs to boundaries, but that mapping rests on the thin evidence above. A senior reader's reply: *"I already put validation and least privilege around anything non-deterministic. Which decision does your three-job map change?"* The draft's objection is "taxonomy for its own sake," and its answer ("the jobs differ in what is at risk") is asserted. The convincing rebuttal is a documented case where misclassification leads to a wrong choice, for example a team applying a component catalog to an operate problem. None is given, so the taxonomy is never shown to be decision-relevant. Checklist question 1 ("Which job is the agent doing?") presumes the answer.
5. **Composition is never addressed.** Real products plausibly combine jobs (an emitted UI, operated by an agent, whose components were written by a coding agent). The draft notes the blur at the project level (A2UI inside MCP Apps) and dismisses it. It never says what the per-job boundaries imply when jobs stack, which is when the boundary map matters most.
6. **The "thin safeguard" objection is waved through.** "Both objections are fair, and neither changes the claim." If a boundary is "easy to misconfigure and silent about content," the cost-benefit of the boundary-first strategy is the real question, and the draft does not weigh it against alternatives such as content evals, output validation or human review. "A thin boundary is a reason to design and test it" does not follow. A thin boundary could equally be a reason to put the effort elsewhere.

## Areas of excessive certainty

- **"That boundary, not the model, is where reliability, accessibility and brand safety get settled."** (The claim; also the thesis.) Certainty far beyond what documentation-only reading can support, and partly contradicted by the draft (see Weakest part). Reduce to what is shown: boundaries limit form and authority.
- **"The permission layer is the boundary"** (Operate). One library, read from its docs, never exercised.
- **"The two views combine without conflict."** (Boundary map, vendor paragraph.) The draft characterizes CopilotKit's spectrum, AG-UI's categories and a community list in one sentence as axes that "measure how much freedom the agent has." This is the claim that carries the originality argument, and it gets one assertion and no demonstration. Either show it for at least one vendor taxonomy or soften it.
- **"Both objections are fair, and neither changes the claim."** Too quick given point 3 and point 6 in the previous section.
- **"Writing code with an agent is the familiar kind of risk."** Asserted. Agent-written UI code at volume, with agents choosing and composing components, may not carry the familiar risk profile. No source is given either way.
- **Overall frame.** The article states the claim as settled while its method section says it is a one-day snapshot of documentation with nothing run. The confidence of the framing does not match the evidence type.

## Sections that add little value

1. **"The claim" section.** It restates the final sentence of the intro paragraph nearly verbatim ("separates three jobs ... argues that in each of them the design decision that matters is the boundary"). Two consecutive statements of the thesis. Keep one, or merge them.
2. **The news peg (the opening two launches and the closing "Two launches, three jobs").** "Within a day of each other" is a date coincidence and does not support the argument. The closing section restates the intro and then lists version news ("1.1 requirements," "AG-UI frozen 1.0," "still shipping betas"), which dates quickly and adds no reasoning. The only new sentence is the last one ("The boundary you draw is the part you own"), which is the best argument for the thesis (the boundary is the durable investment while models churn) but appears with no development. See "How the thesis could be stronger" below.
3. **The json-render code listing, the A2UI message-shape paragraph and the `createAgent` four-line snippet.** For senior engineers, "Zod schema catalog" is understood on sight. The listing serves as illustration, and it does not show a boundary succeeding or failing. Cut, or replace with the one excerpt that matters (see item 1 under Missing counterargument, which the json-render listing happens to contain).
4. **"Transport is a separate question" (AG-UI).** Its job is to say AG-UI is not a category. That is one sentence for the intro or the table footnote, not a subsection.
5. **The MCP Apps host list** (Claude, Claude Desktop, VS Code GitHub Copilot, ...). Does not bear on the boundary argument.
6. **The Write section's description of the skill** ("a workflow, not a prompt," "unglamorous rules"). It is product description, and it is the thinnest evidence in the piece. Either use it to show *how the boundary in Write fails or holds* or reduce it sharply.
7. **"What to ask before you adopt one."** Questions 4 and 5 (check versions across the stack, assess vendor risk) apply to any young open-source dependency; question 3 applies to any security review. Only 1, 2 and 6 are specific to this topic, and 1 and 2 are not yet shown to change a decision. The audience wants "a clear, falsifiable point of view — not a balanced survey" (`config/audience.md`). A checklist ending, with the author-opinion placeholder unfilled, leaves the piece as a survey plus checklist.
8. **"Where the claims outrun the documents" in its current form** (see below). Two of the three items do not support the thesis and read as general due diligence.

### Does "Where the claims outrun the documents" prove the point it is placed to prove?

Only partly. It is placed to support Argument 3 ("a boundary is something to test, not assume") and so to back the thesis.

- **"Stable"**: the vendor claimed a stable *protocol*, and the draft shows the SDKs are 0.x. The draft then concludes "'Stable' describes the protocol's version, not the ecosystem around it," which is what the vendor said. Protocol-1.0-with-0.x-SDKs is common. This is a maturity observation. It is not a claim outrunning a document.
- **"Production"**: the draft puts the word in quotation marks as if the vendors claimed it, then quotes the vendors *disclosing* beta, experimental and shutdown status ("UI SDK is in beta," "currently experimental," "Tambo Cloud is shutting down"). Those are honest disclosures. They show the opposite of outrunning. The Tambo point is vendor risk and says nothing about boundaries.
- **"Sensitive values"** is the only item where a claim about a boundary meets the boundary's documentation, and even there the result is "the pages don't say."

The section therefore shows the field is young and that one privacy claim is not guaranteed. It does not show that boundaries fail or that reliability is decided there. Also note the asymmetry: the draft holds vendors to an evidence standard ("claims need checking before they are relied on") that its own central claim does not meet. The closing sentence of the section ("I found no independent evaluation of the reliability, accessibility or security of generated interfaces") applies to the thesis equally. The thesis is exactly such a reliability claim, and it has no independent evidence either.

**Confirmation bias**: the research looked for overstated claims and boundary gaps. It does not look for cases where vendor claims held up, nor for evidence on the model side that would test "not the model."

### Does the ending add anything?

Not as it stands. It repeats the intro's two-launch frame, adds release-status news, and the one new thought ("Models in these systems change quickly. The boundary you draw is the part you own") is the most defensible form of the thesis. It is a claim about where to invest, not about where reliability is determined, and it comes last with no support. "Watch the boundaries, since they will keep moving" also sits oddly with "the boundary you draw is the part you own": the first refers to specs, the second to your own design, and the draft does not separate them.

### Is emit / write / operate a real insight?

Part real, part relabelling.

- **Real:** emit versus operate. Whether the agent is a *producer* of the interface or a *user* of one that exists changes the risk from "what might be rendered" to "what might be done," and the draft's Operate section is its most original contribution. Mentioning nothing but "constrained" and "unconstrained" would have missed that.
- **Relabelling:** constrained versus unconstrained emit is CopilotKit's controlled / declarative / open-ended spectrum recast as allowlist versus isolation, and the draft says as much when it says the vendor axes "measure how much freedom the agent has."
- **Mixed axes:** emit versus write differ in *when* (runtime versus build time). Operate differs in *what the agent acts on*. The three labels sit on different axes, so "at least three" is not a partition and cannot be checked for completeness. The phrase "at least" makes the taxonomy unfalsifiable by construction.

## How the thesis could be stronger

1. **Narrow to the claim the evidence supports and that a skeptic could disagree with.** For example: *"Every boundary the field offers (component catalogs, sandboxes, permission layers) constrains form or action, none constrains content, so the reliability work that matters is outside the boundary, and the field is not yet providing it."* The draft already contains the evidence: the table's last column and the final Trade-offs paragraph. This is more surprising than "design the boundary," falsifiable (find a documented content control), and fits the audience's wish for a point of view with named trade-offs. Because it changes an approved thesis, it needs re-approval, with the old text kept in git history as before.
2. **Replace "not the model" with a defensible form.** For example: boundaries cap the worst case and are the part you own and keep as models change; model quality and content checks set the typical case. This turns a false dichotomy into a division of labour and makes the last line of the ending the argument. If the author wants to keep the strong form, the draft must offer evidence for it (a documented failure that is attributable to the model not the boundary, or the reverse), and the [AUTHOR OPINION] marker is exactly where the author's view belongs. Do not fill it with invented experience.
3. **State the threat model per job**, one line each: model error, adversarial tool author, adversarial page content. Then the boundary map becomes a map of *which failure each boundary stops*, which is more informative than "where the boundary sits" and tells the reader which job needs which control.
4. **Make the taxonomy earn its place with a decision it changes.** Use a documented case (from the docs, not invented) where choosing a control from the wrong job's playbook gives a gap. Without that, the "taxonomy for its own sake" objection stands.
5. **Decide whether Operate is the headline.** It is the least covered job and the one with the clearest new risk. Two of three jobs resting on one vendor's feature set is a weakness to disclose or fix.
6. **Drop "accessibility and brand safety" from the thesis or argue them.** As written, the draft shows accessibility is decided by the component library, not the boundary around the agent. That is a different claim and arguably a better one (what the boundary lets the agent compose from matters more than the boundary itself).
7. **Rework the ownership clause.** "The boundary the developer owns" fits catalogs and permission layers but not MCP Apps sandboxes. Say who owns each boundary, which is probably what checklist question 2 was reaching for.
8. **Cut or merge the sections listed above** so the new narrower argument is not diluted by product description and release-status news.
