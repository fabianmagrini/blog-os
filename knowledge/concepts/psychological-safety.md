# Psychological safety (Edmondson / Google Project Aristotle)

## What it is

Amy Edmondson (1999) defined team psychological safety as "a shared belief
that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking." Google's Project
Aristotle (2012–2014) studied 180 teams (115 engineering, 65 sales; sizes
3–50, median 9) via hundreds of interviews and 250+ survey items to find
what actually predicts team effectiveness.

## Finding

Psychological safety ranked as the single most important of five factors,
ahead of dependability, structure/clarity, meaning, and impact. Google's own
framing: "what really mattered was less about who is on the team, and more
about how the team worked together."

Important nuance: the mechanism is *learning behavior* (feedback-seeking,
experimenting, discussing errors), not comfort or conflict-avoidance —
psychologically safe teams still disagree openly and hold high standards.

## Why this is a useful comparison point in other articles

This is a large-N, primary-source (Google's own account), *validated*
account of what makes team communication work — useful as a credibility
anchor when discussing less-validated pop-psychology communication tools
(DISC, MBTI, etc.): it shows the actual mechanism that matters is *how a
team interacts*, not *who is on it* or what type each person is labeled.
It does **not** directly test whether a shared naming vocabulary for
communication styles specifically improves outcomes — that remains an
open question, not something this source proves.
